Netflix Ratings App – User-Centered Design Project

User-Centered Design Class 2026

Academic Project

The Netflix Ratings App was a user-centered design project focused on improving how viewers interpret film content ratings. Traditional rating systems provide high-level labels (e.g., PG-13, R) but often lack detailed context about what actually occurs in a film. Our goal was to design a more transparent and informative rating experience that helps users make better viewing decisions.

Responsibilities

  • Conducted user interviews to identify gaps in current rating systems

  • Helped define and refine the “How Might We” problem statement

  • Designed user flows for both viewer and critic roles

  • Developed personas to guide design decisions

  • Created storyboards for primary and secondary users

  • Contributed to paper and digital prototype iterations

  • Incorporated usability feedback into revised interface designs

Problem

Traditional film rating systems are generic and often fail to communicate meaningful details about content. Users expressed that while ratings provide a quick overview, they do not offer enough transparency about:

  • The context of inappropriate content

  • Specific dialogue or actions within scenes

  • When certain content occurs (timestamps)

  • Who is involved in specific scenes

This lack of clarity makes it difficult for viewers — especially families — to determine whether a film aligns with their preferences or values.

So, how might we standardize a new rating system that incorporates detailed content information to give viewers a clearer understanding of what to expect in a film?

Research & Insights

From our initial user interviews, we discovered:

  • Ratings are useful for quick, at-a-glance decisions

  • Users want both summary ratings and detailed breakdowns

  • Timestamped inappropriate content would significantly increase trust

  • Specific descriptions (what was said/done and between whom) matter

  • Ratings strongly influence whether users invest time in watching a film

  • These insights guided our shift toward transparency, specificity, and structured filtering.

Process & Design Evolution


User Flows

We created user flows to visualize two key roles:

  • Primary User – Viewer: Browses ratings, reviews content warnings, filters timestamps

  • Secondary User – Critic/Contributor: Adds or edits detailed scene-based ratings

Mapping these flows helped us define system structure and reduce friction points.


Personas

We narrowed our scope around the viewer persona, since they are the primary decision-maker. Our personas clarified:

  • Motivations (safe viewing, time investment decisions)

  • Frustrations (vague rating categories)

  • Needs (clear, scannable, trustworthy detail)


Storyboarding

We developed two storyboards:

  • Primary (Viewer): Shows navigation through film details, timestamps, and filters

  • Secondary (Critic): Illustrates the process of creating or editing structured reviews

These helped validate task flows before prototyping.


Prototyping → Paper to Digital

We began with paper prototypes to quickly test layout and interaction concepts.

Key changes during iteration:

  • Combined two timestamp screens into one

  • Added filters to improve usability

  • Moved the QR code to improve screen balance

  • Simplified page sections for clarity

  • Introduced pop-ups for detailed scene information

  • Prioritized speed and scannability

User feedback directly influenced our digital prototype refinement, improving clarity and reducing cognitive load.


Reflection

This project reinforced how powerful structured information can be in decision-making systems. By centering our design on real user frustrations, we moved beyond generic ratings and toward meaningful transparency.

I learned the importance of:

  • Designing for clarity and scannability

  • Using personas to narrow scope effectively

  • Iterating from low-fidelity to digital prototypes

  • Treating transparency as a UX feature

This project strengthened my understanding of research-driven design and validated how structured interaction design can meaningfully improve user trust.
